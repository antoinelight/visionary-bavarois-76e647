1. Tree Size & Width (For the Horse)

The tree is the frame of the saddle. Width must match your horse’s back and wither shape so the saddle sits level and does not pinch or bridge.

  • Narrow — thinner, higher withers; common on some Thoroughbreds and gaited horses
  • Regular / Medium — average Quarter Horse / stock-type backs
  • Wide / Extra-Wide — broader, flatter backs and lower withers

If the tree is too narrow, the saddle perches high and can dig into the withers. If it is too wide, it sits too low and may put pressure on the spine or rock.

2. Gullet Clearance

There should be clear space over the withers when the rider is mounted. You should be able to fit a few fingers under the pommel/gullet without the saddle pressing down. Pads help, but they cannot fix a tree that is fundamentally the wrong width.

3. Saddle Positioning

Place the saddle so the tree points sit behind the shoulder blade, not on it. The saddle should look level from side to side and front to back when the horse is standing square. A saddle that is too far forward restricts the shoulder; too far back can put weight on the loins.

4. Hard-to-Fit Horses

  • High withers — need more gullet clearance and often a narrower or more cut-back tree
  • Short backs — shorter skirts and careful bar length so the saddle does not dig into the loins
  • Very wide or mutton-withered — wide or extra-wide trees; avoid saddles that sit low on the spine
  • Asymmetry — uneven muscling or old injuries; photos from both sides help us advise

5. Signs of Poor Fit

  • White hairs, dry spots, or swelling under the saddle area
  • Horse hollows, pins ears, or moves away when you girth up
  • Saddle slides, rocks, or leaves uneven sweat marks
  • Rider feels tipped forward or backward, or has trouble sitting centered

6. Seat Size (For the Rider)

Seat size is measured in inches and is about your comfort and balance, not the horse. A seat that is too small feels cramped; too large can leave you sliding and less secure.

Quick Seat Chart (Western)

  • 14" – 14.5" — youth / smaller adults
  • 15" – 15.5" — average adult
  • 16" – 16.5" — larger adult / preferred by many trail riders
  • 17"+ — larger riders or those who want extra room

When in doubt, many adults prefer a 15.5" or 16" Western seat for all-day comfort. English seat sizes run differently (often in the 16.5"–18" range for adults).

7. Saddles by Discipline

  • Trail / pleasure — comfort-focused, often deeper seats, good weight distribution
  • Ranch / work — durable trees, practical skirts, secure seat
  • Arena / reining / cutting — specific tree and seat designs for the discipline
  • English (dressage, hunt, all-purpose) — panel contact and balance matter more; fit is less “forgiving”

8. How to Buy a Saddle — Quick Checklist

  1. Know your horse’s approximate tree need (or send us photos).
  2. Know your preferred seat size and riding style.
  3. Decide budget and whether you want new or used.
  4. Check return / exchange policy and shipping.
  5. Ask about trade-in if you already own a saddle that no longer fits.

9. What Is a Saddle Tree?

The tree is the internal frame (wood, fiberglass, or composite) that gives the saddle its shape and spreads the rider’s weight. “Flex trees” flex slightly under load; traditional trees are more rigid. Both can fit well when the width and bar shape match the horse.

  • Western saddles distribute weight over a larger area and are often more forgiving of minor fit issues, but tree width is still critical for long rides.
  • English saddles require more precise panel contact. A poor fit shows up quickly in the canter or over fences.

10. Measuring Tips

For the horse: Photos from the side (showing wither height and back length) and from above (looking down the spine) are the most useful for remote advice. Note if the horse is in full work or turned out, and any previous saddle issues.

Girth / cinch: Measure from the middle of the saddle’s billet area under the belly to the same point on the other side, or use your current cinch as a starting point. Leave room for a pad and for the horse to expand when breathing.

11. Saddle Care & When to Re-check Fit

  • Clean sweat and dirt after rides; condition leather periodically so it stays supple.
  • Squeaks often come from dry leather or fender/stirrup leather friction — clean and condition, and check for worn hardware.
  • Horses change shape with age, season, fitness, and weight. Re-check fit if you see white hairs, sore spots, girthing resistance, or a change in movement.
  • We accept trade-ins when it is time to move to a different tree or seat size.
